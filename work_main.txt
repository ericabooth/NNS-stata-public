     Abstract.     The nnsreg command fits a piecewise-linear regression curve using
     the partition-based approach of Nonlinear Nonparametric Statistics (Viole and
     Nawrocki 2013), previously available to R users in the NNS package (Viole 2026).
     The command recursively partitions the data using partial-moment quadrants,
     computes a regression point inside each partition, and posts the connected seg-
     ments as a linear spline with data-derived knots. Because the fit is posted as ordi-
     nary estimation output, users can apply predict, estimates table, lincom, and
     coefficient plots to report where a fitted relationship bends, which ranges are flat
     or steep, and whether that pattern survives a change in tuning options. Three sim-
     ulated examples and a 100-replication Monte Carlo study compare the command
     with linear regression, npregress kernel, and npregress series. On smooth
     curves the two npregress estimators recover the true curve more accurately on
     average. When observations cluster around sharp local features, a least-squares
     variant, method(ols), which keeps the NNS-derived knots but fits the segment
     coefficients by least squares, nearly matches the most accurate smoother on aver-
     age and is far more stable across replications. The simulated examples use known
     generating curves so the comparison can score signal recovery; a real-data illustra-
     tion on the motorcycle-impact benchmark, scored by cross-validation, shows the
     same pattern out of sample, where the least-squares fit ties the kernel smoother
     and reports segment slopes it does not.
     Keywords: st0000, nnsreg, nonparametric regression, partial moment, linear spline,
     knot, postestimation



1    Introduction
Where does a relationship change? The applied analyst who asks this question, in
education finance, program evaluation, or health-services research, usually needs less a
picture of the relationship than a few reproducible statements about where it is flat,
steep, or changing. Test outcomes that stop improving after spending passes a level,
infrastructure that deteriorates slowly until a threshold age and quickly afterward, or
risk that accelerates only in the tail are each a question about where a fitted curve
bends and how steep it is on either side. A single regression slope cannot answer them,
because it averages over the whole range of the data. A flexible smoother such as
npregress kernel (StataCorp 2025) can display the shape, but its output is a curve,
and the analyst must do additional work, with margins or by hand, to turn it into a
small set of reportable slope statements. Making a flexible fit easy to report is itself

a recognized Stata problem: xblc tabulates and plots flexible covariate fits (Orsini
and Greenland 2011), and marginscontplot plots the marginal effects of continuous
predictors (Royston 2013). nnsreg works in that tradition, and returns a different
object: named ranges and slopes as ordinary estimation results.
    Stata already offers several routes to a nonlinear fit, and nnsreg sits among them
rather than replacing them. mkspline builds a linear spline but asks the analyst to set
the knots, either by hand or at sample percentiles, which spread the knots by rank rather
than by the data’s own structure; placing knots adaptively, from the data rather than by
rank, is a long-standing idea in the spline literature (Friedman 1991). Polynomials and
fractional polynomials (Royston and Altman 1994) bend smoothly but do not name
the places where the slope changes. npregress series fits a spline or polynomial
basis with a data-driven number of terms and posts estimation results, and npregress
kernel traces a smooth mean. The community-contributed binsreg partitions the
regressor’s support into data-driven quantile bins and fits within each bin, with formal
inference (Cattaneo et al. 2024, 2025). And when the question is the location of one or a
few breaks, with inference on where they lie, formal segmented-regression and joinpoint
estimators (Muggeo 2003; Kim et al. 2000), and Stata’s own threshold command,
answer it directly; nnsreg is an exploratory, descriptive complement to those, not a
substitute. What nnsreg adds is a particular way of placing the segment boundaries,
the NNS partition of the data, paired with coefficients named for the first slope and
each later slope change.
   nnsreg takes its way of placing boundaries from an established method. Nonlinear
Nonparametric Statistics (NNS), developed by Viole and Nawrocki (2013), builds re-
gression fits from partial moments, which are averages of deviations below and above
a target value (Viole and Nawrocki 2012a,b; Nawrocki and Viole 2024). The NNS
regression algorithm recursively partitions the scatterplot into quadrants, computes a
representative regression point inside each partition, and connects the points with line
segments. Vinod and Viole (2018b) compare this approach with kernel regression from
the R np package (Hayfield and Racine 2008) and report three practical advantages: the
method requires no bandwidth choice, the segment slopes estimate derivatives directly,
and the partitions adapt to data whose support is clustered rather than spread evenly.
They also report that in-sample fit is similar for the two approaches, with differences
they describe as minor. A companion paper (Vinod and Viole 2018a) describes the NNS
regression points as analogues of linear-spline knots. The reference implementation is
the NNS package for R (Viole 2026).
    This is a software and workflow contribution, not a new estimator. First, nnsreg
brings the univariate core of NNS regression to Stata as a deliberate subset of the R
package (section 2.3 lists the differences). Second, and to my mind the more useful
part for applied work, nnsreg posts its fit as ordinary Stata estimation results. The
first-segment slope and each subsequent change in slope are named coefficients in e(b),
the knot locations are stored in e(knots), and predict, estimates store, lincom,
and coefplot (Jann 2014) work as they do after regress. Posting a nonstandard fit
as ordinary results so that the usual postestimation tools apply is a familiar way to
build a Stata command (Newson 2006). A threshold claim such as “the fitted slope is

flat below age 35 and about 1.2 points per year at the oldest ages” becomes a pair of
lincom statements rather than a reading exercise on a graph.
    nnsreg is descriptive: a bend in a fitted curve is a feature of the conditional mean
in the sample, not a causal threshold, and causal interpretation still requires a research
design. Nor is nnsreg a general replacement for npregress kernel: in my simulations
the kernel smoother recovers smooth curves more accurately on average, and I say so
throughout. The command earns its place when the data are clustered or gapped, or
when the deliverable is a segment-level summary with standard postestimation tools.
    Section 2 explains how the method works. Section 3 documents the syntax, op-
tions, and stored results. Section 4 compares the command with linear regression
and npregress kernel and npregress series on three simulated designs with known
truth, including a Monte Carlo study across 100 replications. Section 5 repeats the
comparison on the real motorcycle-impact data, scored by cross-validation. Section 6
offers guidance on when to use which tool, and section 7 summarizes the findings and
the command’s limits.


2     The NNS regression approach
2.1    Partial moments and recursive partitioning
The estimation problem is the usual one: we observe pairs (xi , yi ) and want the con-
ditional mean of y as a function of x without committing to a functional form. The
NNS approach starts from partial moments, which summarize a variable’s behavior on
one side of a target value t. One-sided moments of this kind are an established tool
with a five-decade history in risk measurement (Bawa 1975; Fishburn 1977); the NNS
contribution is to pair targets on both variables of a bivariate sample and to build a
regression method on the resulting quadrants. The lower and upper partial moments of
degree r are
                                           N
                                        1 X
                         LPMr (t; X) =        max(0, t − Xi )r
                                        N i=1
                                             N
                                         1 X
                         UPMr (t; X) =         max(0, Xi − t)r
                                         N i=1

At degree zero these are simply the shares of observations below and above t, so partial
moments generalize the empirical distribution function (Viole and Nawrocki 2012a).
The useful fact for regression is that a pair of targets, one for X and one for Y , divides
the scatterplot into four quadrants, and those quadrants describe the local joint behavior
of the two variables (Viole and Nawrocki 2012b).
   The algorithm applies this idea recursively. Starting from the full sample, nnsreg
computes a center for X and a center for Y (the mean by default, the median with
noise(median)), splits the observations into the four quadrants around that center, and
then repeats the split inside every quadrant that still holds more than eight observations.
4                                                  nnsreg: Reportable segment slopes

Splitting therefore reaches more finely into dense regions than into sparse ones. The
order() option caps how many rounds of splitting are allowed, so a higher order permits
a more finely segmented fit; the default of three balances resolution against noise across
the designs studied here (section 4.6). With partition(xonly), the command instead
splits only along X, which produces vertical bands; this variant is useful as a sensitivity
check and for applications where partitioning on the outcome is undesirable.


2.2    From regression points to a linear spline
After partitioning stops, nnsreg collapses each final group to a single regression point,
its center in (x, y) space. It then adds two boundary points at the smallest and largest
observed x (estimated from local linear fits near each boundary), adds a central point,
sorts all points by x, and connects them. The connected segments are then expressed as
a linear spline. With interior knots k1 , . . . , km placed at the interior regression points,
the fitted function is
                                                  m
                                                  X
                            fb(x) = β0 + β1 x +         γj (x − kj )+
                                                  j=1


where (x − kj )+ = max(0, x − kj ). The coefficient β1 is the slope of the first segment,
and each γj is the change in slope at knot kj . This representation follows Vinod and
Viole (2018a), who describe NNS regression points as playing the role that knots play
in a linear spline; the (x − kj )+ basis itself is the standard truncated-line representation
of a linear spline in semiparametric regression (Ruppert et al. 2003). It is also what
makes the method convenient in Stata: a piecewise-linear curve is exactly a regression
on a spline basis, so the fit can be posted, stored, and manipulated with standard tools.
    nnsreg offers two ways to assign coefficients to that basis. The default, method(connect),
chooses the coefficients so that the spline passes through the NNS regression points; this
is the construction closest to the NNS literature. The alternative, method(ols), keeps
the NNS-derived knots but estimates the spline coefficients by ordinary least squares.
This least-squares refinement is a natural extension rather than part of the original
NNS construction: it borrows the NNS knot locations and nothing else. The connected
fit summarizes the partition structure itself; the OLS fit uses the same segmentation
but minimizes squared error given the knots, which in my simulations recovers the true
curve more accurately (section 4).
     The reported standard errors deserve a word of caution. For method(ols), they
are the usual least-squares ones, conditional on the data-derived knot locations. For
method(connect), nnsreg rescales the OLS spline covariance matrix by the connected
fit’s residual variance. Neither version accounts for the fact that the knots were chosen
from the same data; inference that does account for an estimated break or kink location
is available for segmented models with one or a few knots (Muggeo 2003; Hansen 2017),
and extending it to the NNS partition is beyond this article. I therefore recommend
reading these intervals as descriptive summaries of segment stability rather than as
exact inference; the sensitivity tools in section 4.6 are the more trustworthy check.

2.3    Relationship to the R NNS package
The NNS package for R (Viole 2026) is the reference implementation of this method,
and nnsreg deliberately implements a subset of it. Users moving between the two
should know the differences. The R function NNS.reg supports multivariate regressors,
classification-style prediction, and automatic selection of the partition depth based on
an internal dependence measure interpreted as a signal-to-noise ratio (Vinod and Viole
2018a). nnsreg is univariate, uses a fixed user-chosen order(), and offers mean or
median partition centers. In exchange, it integrates with Stata’s estimation machinery:
e-class results, predict in and out of the estimation sample, and compatibility with
estimates, lincom, and coefplot. Users who need multivariate NNS regression or
automated order selection should use the R package; users whose deliverable is a Stata
workflow with reportable segment coefficients are the audience for nnsreg.1

where xb (the default) computes fitted values from the stored spline and residuals

3.2    Options
order(#) sets the partition depth, that is, how many rounds of recursive splitting are
   allowed. A higher order permits more line segments, which can follow curvature
   more closely but can also track noise. The default is order(3). Section 4.6 shows
   how to read a small grid of orders as a sensitivity check.
noise(mean | median) sets the center used for partitioning and for the regression
   points. noise(mean), the default, uses arithmetic means. noise(median) uses
   medians, which move less when a partition contains outlying or skewed values.
method(connect | ols) sets how spline coefficients are assigned. method(connect),
   the default, interpolates the NNS regression points and is the construction closest
   to the NNS literature. method(ols) keeps the NNS-derived knots and estimates the
   spline coefficients by ordinary least squares, which typically fits the observed data
   more closely.
partition(xy | xonly) sets the partition style. partition(xy), the default, splits on
   both variables in quadrants, following the NNS construction. partition(xonly)
   splits only along the regressor, producing vertical bands, and serves as a sensitivity
   check.
minobs(#) sets the smallest group the recursive partition keeps splitting: a group is
   split only if it holds more than # observations. The default is minobs(8). Larger
   values yield fewer, longer segments and make a useful sensitivity check alongside
   order().
level(#) sets the confidence level for the reported intervals; the default is level(95)
   or as set by set level.
vce(vcetype) passes a variance estimator through to the least-squares stage; vcetype
   may be robust or cluster clustvar. Because it re-weights an ordinary least-squares
   covariance, it is allowed only with method(ols). The resulting standard errors are
   robust or clustered but remain conditional on the data-derived knots.
slopes reports a table of per-segment slopes, each the cumulative sum of the spline
   coefficients up to that segment, with delta-method standard errors. These are the
   quantities the lincom statements below compute by hand; the same table is stored
   in e(segments).
noplot suppresses the default scatter-and-fit graph.
knotlines overlays vertical dashed lines at the interior knot locations on the default
   graph.
graph opts(twoway options) passes options through to the default graph.
generate(newvar ) creates a variable holding the fitted values over the estimation sam-
   ple.

4     Simulated comparisons
4.1    Design: three known curves, one seed, and 100 replications
All examples are simulated so that the true conditional mean is known, which lets us
score each method on how well it recovers the curve that generated the data rather than
only on how well it fits noise. The variable names borrow from Texas policy settings
(school spending and a test-passing rate, water-system pipe age and water loss) to keep
the examples concrete. The names are labels of convenience: all values are synthetic
and stylized, and no claim in this article is about actual districts, assessments, or water
systems.
   The replication script test nnsreg.do, included with this article, generates every
number and figure shown here from fixed seeds. It creates three designs:

    • Education (smooth S-curve). For 250 districts, spending per pupil x is uni-
      form on [5, 20] (thousands of dollars) and the true passing rate is 40 + 45/{1 +
      exp(−0.8[x − 11])}, an S-curve that is flat at low spending, rises through the
      middle, and plateaus near the top. Noise is normal with standard deviation 4.

    • Water (threshold). For 300 systems, pipe age a is uniform on [0, 80] years and
      the true loss rate is flat at 8% for a ≤ 35 and 8 + 0.4(a − 35) + 0.01(a − 35)2
      afterward, so the curve has a kink at age 35. Noise has standard deviation 2.5.

    • Clustered (peak and dip). For 260 units, the score s is drawn from a three-
      component normal mixture (means 22, 36, and 58 with standard deviations 3,
      2.2, and 4; mixture weights 0.45, 0.30, and 0.25), so the support is dense in three
      regions and thin between them. The true curve is 18+0.18s+10 g24,35 −16 g35,18 +
      8 g58,50 , where gc,w = exp{−(s − c)2 /w}, which produces a local peak near 24, a
E. A. Booth                                                                              9

      sharp dip near 35, and a partial recovery near 58. Noise has standard deviation
      1.2. This design mirrors the clustered-data setting that Vinod and Viole (2018b)
      identify as favorable to partition-based fits.

    Each design has a canonical dataset with a fixed seed (2036, 2037, and 2038), used
for the figures and the single-run table. Because any single draw can flatter one method
by luck, the script also redraws each design 100 times with fresh seeds recorded in the
script, refits every method, and stores the results; section 4.5 reports that study.
    Three statistics summarize each fit. In-sample R2 and RMSE against the observed
outcome measure conformity to the data, including its noise. RMSE against the true
curve, available only because the data are simulated, measures recovery of the signal.
The distinction matters: a method can score better on observed RMSE by chasing noise
while scoring worse on the truth, and the clustered example below shows exactly that
pattern. Table 1 collects the single-run diagnostics for all three examples; the discussion
of each example refers to its rows.


4.2    Example 1: on a smooth S-curve, the kernel smoother wins on
       average and the spline stays close
The education design asks whether each method can represent a plateau without the
analyst choosing a polynomial order or hand-picking knots. Figure 1 shows four of the
fits, one per panel, so each curve can be read against the data without overplotting;
npregress series appears in table 1 rather than in the figure. The linear fit misses
both the flat start and the plateau, and its true-curve RMSE of 5.460 is six to seven
times that of the smoothers and the NNS OLS fit. The kernel smoother tracks the S-
curve closely (true-curve RMSE 0.923) and npregress series slightly closer (0.811).
The NNS OLS spline is closest in this particular draw (0.784), though the Monte Carlo
study below shows the two npregress fits ahead on average; the connected NNS fit is
visibly more jagged (3.283) because its coefficients interpolate regression points instead
of minimizing squared error.

6    Practical guidance
The simulations support a simple division of labor. Consider npregress kernel or
npregress series when the goal is the most accurate estimate of a smooth conditional
mean and the sample covers the range of interest evenly; the two were the most accurate
methods in both smooth designs, and npregress series is the closest competitor to
24                                               nnsreg: Reportable segment slopes

nnsreg because it too posts a data-driven spline as estimation results. When the goal is
instead formal inference on a small number of break locations, a segmented or threshold
estimator is the right tool, not nnsreg.
     Consider nnsreg when the deliverable is a segment-level summary: named slope co-
efficients, knot locations in e(knots), and lincom-testable contrasts such as the plateau
and threshold tests above, none of which npregress reports. The command is also the
better choice when the regressor’s support is clustered or gapped: there npregress
series can reach a low average error but swings widely from draw to draw, while
method(ols) delivers about the same average accuracy with a fraction of the draw-to-
draw spread. Reach for method(ols) when a reliable fit matters more than the best-case
fit. (The default connected fit is the descriptive, partition-faithful variant; method(ols)
is the accuracy-competitive one.) And because the two approaches’ tuning choices fail in
different ways, a bandwidth-free nnsreg fit is a useful alternative representation along-
side a kernel or series fit. The value there is triangulation: when a kernel smoother and
the NNS partition both show a plateau or a trough, the feature is less likely an artifact of
one smoothing choice; when they disagree, the shape is sensitive to tuning and is better
reported as tentative. Bandwidth-free is not tuning-free, though: order(), minobs(),
and the partition choices control complexity in the bandwidth’s place, so the sensitivity
grid of section 4.6 does the work a bandwidth search would. Table 4 lays the contrasts
side by side.

    Results from nnsreg need the same scrutiny as any data-driven fit. Fit a small
grid of order() values and both centers, and report whether the shape survives, as
in section 4.6. And when prediction accuracy is the question, compare methods on a
common metric that penalizes noise-chasing, such as the held-out cross-validation error
of the motorcycle example in section 5; the true-curve RMSE used with the simulated
designs is the same idea when the generating curve is known. Turning to computation,
each example in this article fits in well under a second, and the full 100-replication Monte
Carlo study, including 300 npregress kernel fits, completes in under three minutes
on a desktop; I make no stronger computational claims.

7    Conclusion
nnsreg adapts the NNS regression method of Viole and Nawrocki (2013) to Stata and,
more importantly for daily work, makes its output a full participant in Stata’s estimation
ecosystem. The simulations place the command honestly: the npregress estimators re-
cover smooth curves more accurately on average, while on the clustered, sharply featured
designs studied here the NNS OLS spline nearly matches the most accurate smoother
on average and is far more stable across samples. In every design it yields a compact
segment summary, with reportable slopes and their conditional standard errors, that a
smooth curve does not.
    The package’s limits define its future work. The command is univariate and un-
weighted. Its interval estimates are conditional on data-derived knots, and because the
number and location of knots change from resample to resample, a coefficient-wise boot-
strap is not meaningful; the natural upgrade is a bootstrap that repeats the partitioning
and summarizes quantities every resample shares, such as the fitted curve at fixed grid
points or slopes at fixed quantiles of the regressor. And it asks users to choose order()
by comparison rather than automating the choice; the dependence-based order selection
in the R package (Viole 2026) and cross-validation over a small order() grid are the
obvious models for a future order(auto) option. I would rather add those pieces after
they can be validated than imply more automation than the command has today.
